SUBMISSION: 18
TITLE: Phizura: On-the-Fly Music Recording with Method Proxies

----------- Overall evaluation -----------
SCORE: 3 (strong accept)
----- TEXT:
I really enjoyed this paper. A real tool, a real need, and a playful idea.
My main observation is that the paper undersells its strongest claim. In one line: live music in live Pharo. Phizura can exist because Pharo is a fully live environment — instrumentation can be added and removed at runtime during a performance, and the replay is not a video or log but live, editable code. The recording itself becomes an instrument. This point is implicit throughout but never stated explicitly. I encourage you to make it so. Your own group’s recent “It’s Alive!” paper (arXiv:2603.02987) provides the perfect reference for the live-object foundation.
The abstract is long because the paper touches several interesting things at once: a recorder for live music performances, a creative use of Method Proxies, and a general mechanism for capturing interactive sessions. I suggest choosing one of these as the main driver and letting the others appear as valuable side effects. The reader should know from the first paragraph which journey they are on.
A concrete suggestion: include a short audible appendix. A 5-minute video showing a live session followed by replay of the emitted script (with timestamps) would make the contribution much more tangible. Publishing the emitted script alongside the video would also align with the paper’s own argument that performances can be shared as lightweight text files.
Smaller points: The MoofLOD mode needs one sentence explaining its intended consumers. The planned API for user-defined handlers is exciting — one concrete example would help the community see its potential. Finally, the text would benefit from a careful proofreading pass (duplicated sentence in Section 6, repeated future-work paragraph, missing periods, and footnote typos for Coypu and Phizura).
The MoofLOD mode would benefit from one sentence explaining what MoofLOD is.
The limitations paragraph is refreshingly honest. This is exactly the kind of working-system paper I like to see at IWST. I would be happy to review a revised version.

The submission introduces Phizura as a live coding platform for music performance, producing and recording. One of its contributions is integration with the Method Proxies library that improves the design, adds significant features and increases reusability.

The paper is well written and easy to follow. However, to convince the reader about the improvements by the new approach, I would suggest adding some illustrations of "before and after". Similarly, it would be very nice to see some examples in the section "Generality beyond music".

As a minor remark, please be sure to add all important references. For example, Block and Roassal are mentioned without a real explanation and, more importantly, a reference.


----------------------- REVIEW 3 ---------------------

SUBMISSION: 18
TITLE: Phizura: On-the-Fly Music Recording with Method Proxies

----------- Overall evaluation -----------
SCORE: 3 (strong accept)
----- TEXT:
The technology described in this article constitutes a real contribution. By refactoring previous work in depth, the authors achieve increased flexibility, better performance, and also a wider range of use cases, which brings clear added value. The scope of the work is therefore significantly improved, with the possibility for new users, coming with their own libraries, to exploit and benefit from the proposed environment.

The notion of live coding is first introduced as a real-time activity, mainly in a musical context. The idea of lightweight recording of coding activities in transcripts, with timestamps and replay capabilities, appears to provide a sort of sandbox for many experiments. Especially as the Method Proxies approach largely eliminates coupling between the recording
infrastructure and the domain library.

Since the authors claim that scenarios can be replayed, it should also be possible to refactor them. It would be interesting to relate this to a form of debugging applied to musical expression, in the sense of a performer repeating a piece until reaching a satisfying level of mastery.
This suggests that live coding is not only about real-time performance, but also about revisiting and incrementally improving what has been produced.

Another aspect that could be explored is the level of expressivity. For instance, trying to reproduce well-known electronic music tracks, even in simplified versions (e.g., Waiting for the Stars by Vitalic), would help better assess the capabilities of the system. It could also be a motivating exercise for students.

I believe one other interesting future orientation could be to consider the infrastructure as a kind of digital twin for robotics (sending timestamped commands), with concurrent live coding opening space for team-based contest organization (e.g., moving a robot along a predefined path, performing actions while playing music and strobing light effects).
